Introduction to Mechanics

Mechanics is a branch of physics that deals with the motion of objects and the forces responsible for producing or changing that motion. It helps us understand how objects move, why they stop, and how energy and momentum are transferred.

Importance of Mechanics

  • Helps explain the motion of everyday objects.
  • Provides the foundation for engineering and technology.
  • Explains the movement of vehicles and machines.
  • Helps us understand energy transfer and collisions.
  • Has applications in laboratory experiments and scientific research.

Main Topics in This Unit

  2. Force and motion in inertial frames
  3. Friction and air resistance
  4. Work-energy theorem and applications

1. Kinematics Using Calculus

Kinematics is the branch of mechanics that describes the motion of objects without considering the forces causing the motion. It studies displacement, velocity and acceleration.

1.1 Position and Displacement

Position describes the location of an object relative to a reference point. Displacement is the change in position of an object in a particular direction.

Displacement: Δx = x₂ − x₁

Displacement is a vector quantity because it has both magnitude and direction.

1.2 Distance and Displacement

DistanceDisplacement
Total path travelled.Change in position.
Scalar quantity.Vector quantity.
Always zero or positive.Can be positive, negative or zero in one dimension.

1.3 Velocity

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement with respect to time.

Average velocity = Displacement / Time
Instantaneous velocity: v = dx/dt

In calculus, velocity is obtained by differentiating the position function with respect to time.

1.4 Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with respect to time.

Average acceleration = (v₂ − v₁) / (t₂ − t₁)
Instantaneous acceleration: a = dv/dt = d²x/dt²

1.5 Motion Equations

For motion with constant acceleration:

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as
s = (u + v)t / 2

Where:

  • u = Initial velocity
  • v = Final velocity
  • a = Acceleration
  • t = Time
  • s = Displacement

1.6 Worked Example

Question: A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 2 m/s² for 5 seconds. Find its final velocity.

Given:

u = 0 m/s, a = 2 m/s², t = 5 s

Formula:

v = u + at

Substitution:

v = 0 + (2 × 5)

Answer:

v = 10 m/s

1.7 Position-Time Graph

The slope of a position-time graph represents velocity. A steeper slope indicates a greater speed.

Key idea: Differentiation helps us obtain velocity from position and acceleration from velocity.

2. Force and Motion in Inertial Frames

Force is a push or pull that can change the state of motion or the shape of an object. Newton's laws explain the relationship between force and motion.

2.1 Inertial Frame of Reference

An inertial frame is a reference frame that is at rest or moving with constant velocity. Newton's laws of motion are valid in such a frame without introducing fictitious forces.

2.2 Newton's First Law

An object remains at rest or continues moving with uniform velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a net external force.

ΣF = 0 → Constant velocity

This law is also called the law of inertia.

2.3 Newton's Second Law

The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass.

ΣF = ma

Where F is force in newtons, m is mass in kilograms and a is acceleration in m/s².

2.4 Newton's Third Law

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. These forces act on different objects.

Example: When a person walks, the foot pushes the ground backward. The ground exerts a forward frictional force on the person, helping them move forward.

2.5 Free-Body Diagram

A free-body diagram represents all the external forces acting on a selected object.

2.6 Worked Example

Question: A 5 kg object experiences a net force of 20 N. Find its acceleration. Formula: a = F/m Substitution: a = 20/5 Answer: a = 4 m/s²

3. Friction and Air Resistance

Friction is a force that opposes the relative motion or tendency of motion between two surfaces in contact. It acts parallel to the surfaces.

3.1 Types of Friction

TypeExplanation
Static frictionPrevents an object from starting to slide.
Kinetic frictionActs when surfaces slide against each other.
Rolling frictionOpposes the rolling motion of an object.

3.2 Static Friction

Static friction adjusts its magnitude to prevent sliding, up to a maximum value.

fₛ ≤ μₛN

3.3 Kinetic Friction

Kinetic friction acts when an object is sliding over a surface.

fₖ = μₖN

Where μ is the coefficient of friction and N is the normal reaction force.

3.4 Air Resistance

Air resistance is a force that opposes the motion of an object moving through air. It is also known as drag.

The magnitude of drag depends on factors such as speed, shape, size and properties of the surrounding air.

3.5 Terminal Velocity

When an object falls through air, its speed increases and air resistance generally increases. Eventually, the drag force balances the object's weight. The net force becomes zero, and the object continues falling at constant velocity. This is called terminal velocity.

At terminal velocity: Weight = Air resistance

3.6 Worked Example

Question: A 10 kg box rests on a horizontal surface. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2. Find the kinetic frictional force. Take g = 9.8 m/s². Step 1: N = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N Step 2: fₖ = μₖN fₖ = 0.2 × 98 Answer: fₖ = 19.6 N
Application: Friction allows vehicles to move, helps people walk and provides grip between objects. However, excessive friction can cause energy loss and wear.

4. Work-Energy Theorem and Applications

Work is done when a force causes displacement of an object. Energy is the capacity to do work.

4.1 Work Done

When a constant force acts on an object and produces displacement, work is calculated using:

W = Fs cos θ

Where F is force, s is displacement and θ is the angle between force and displacement.

4.2 Types of Work

  • Positive work: Force and displacement act in the same general direction.
  • Negative work: Force acts opposite to displacement.
  • Zero work: No displacement occurs, or the force is perpendicular to displacement.

4.3 Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion.

KE = ½mv²

4.4 Potential Energy

Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object possesses because of its position or height.

PE = mgh

4.5 Work-Energy Theorem

The net work done on an object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.

Wnet = ΔKE = KEfinal − KEinitial

4.6 Conservation of Mechanical Energy

When only conservative forces act, the total mechanical energy remains constant.

KE + PE = Constant

4.7 Energy Transformation

Energy can change from one form to another. For example, when a roller coaster moves down a track, gravitational potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases.

4.8 Worked Example

Question: A 2 kg object is lifted to a height of 5 m. Calculate its gravitational potential energy. Take g = 9.8 m/s². Formula: PE = mgh Substitution: PE = 2 × 9.8 × 5 Answer: PE = 98 J

4.9 Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

P = W/t

SI unit: Watt (W).

5. Impulse and Conservation of Momentum in Collisions

5.1 Momentum

Momentum is the product of the mass and velocity of an object. It is a vector quantity.

p = mv

SI unit: kg m/s.

5.2 Impulse

Impulse is the change in momentum of an object. It is produced when a force acts over a period of time.

J = FΔt = Δp

5.3 Conservation of Momentum

The law of conservation of momentum states that the total momentum of an isolated system remains constant if the net external force acting on the system is zero.

Total momentum before collision = Total momentum after collision
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

5.4 Types of Collisions

Elastic collisionInelastic collision
Momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved. Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not conserved.
Objects may separate after collision. Some kinetic energy changes into other forms.

5.5 Perfectly Inelastic Collision

In a perfectly inelastic collision, the two objects stick together after collision and move with a common velocity. Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not.

v = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂) / (m₁ + m₂)

5.6 Worked Example

Question: A 2 kg trolley moving at 4 m/s collides with a stationary 2 kg trolley. They stick together. Find their common velocity. Given: m₁ = 2 kg, u₁ = 4 m/s m₂ = 2 kg, u₂ = 0 m/s Formula: v = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁ + m₂) Substitution: v = (2 × 4 + 2 × 0)/(2 + 2) v = 8/4 Answer: v = 2 m/s

5.7 Applications of Momentum

  • Vehicle collisions and safety systems.
  • Rocket propulsion.
  • Sports involving balls and moving objects.
  • Recoil and motion of objects.
  • Air-track collision experiments in physics laboratories.

Critical Thinking and Real-Life Applications

1. Why do passengers move forward when a bus suddenly stops?

Because of inertia. The passenger's body tends to continue moving forward even when the bus slows down. This demonstrates Newton's first law of motion.

2. Why are vehicle tyres designed with grooves?

Grooves help improve grip by allowing water to move away from the contact area between the tyre and road. This helps reduce the risk of losing traction.

3. Why does a parachute reduce the speed of a falling person?

A parachute increases the surface area exposed to air. This increases drag and reduces the terminal velocity.

4. Why do airbags help protect passengers?

Airbags increase the time over which a passenger's momentum changes during a collision. For a similar change in momentum, increasing the time reduces the average force.

5. Why does a moving object eventually stop on a rough surface?

Friction acts opposite to the direction of motion. It transfers mechanical energy into thermal energy, causing the object to slow down and eventually stop.
